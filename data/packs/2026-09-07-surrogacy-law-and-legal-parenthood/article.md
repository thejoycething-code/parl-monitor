# MPs from across the House speak out against surrogacy law change

*Draft news article, 8 September 2026. Every quotation is the Hansard wording; each speaker's name links to their contribution. The closing spokesperson paragraph is a placeholder for CitizenGO to approve and attribute before publication.*

---

MPs from Labour, the Conservatives and the DUP have spoken out against a petition calling for intended parents in surrogacy arrangements to be recognised as a child's legal parents from the moment of birth, warning that the change would strip away protections for surrogate mothers and treat babies as "goods to be ordered".

The debate in Westminster Hall on Monday 7 September was triggered by e-petition 763161, which gathered more than 113,000 signatures. Under current law the woman who gives birth is the child's legal mother, and intended parents must apply to the family court for a parental order between six weeks and six months after the birth, with an independent welfare assessment by the Children and Family Court Advisory and Support Service (CAFCASS). The petition describes that law as "outdated".

Eight MPs spoke against the proposed change, several of them at length, against four who argued for reform along the lines of the Law Commission's 2023 report. Responding for the Government, Public Health Minister Dame Diana Johnson said ministers were "not in a position to bring forward legislation on surrogacy reform immediately" and would respond to the Law Commission "in due course as time allows".

## "Pregnant women are not factories"

[Jonathan Hinder](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-A1C84C1B-BAD0-4F0E-A1FF-590E2C06A770), the Labour MP for Pendle and Clitheroe, delivered the most direct condemnation of the petition, taking aim at its own wording.

> "The text of the petition says that the current law is 'outdated' because the woman giving birth to the child is recognised as the legal mother 'even with no biological connection or intention to parent the child.' Just think about how cold and clinical those words are: 'no biological connection', when that woman carried that child in their body and brought that child into this world. That is the commodification of women's bodies. To carry a child for nine months, share your body, feel new life kicking inside you and endure the trauma and joy of childbirth—those are experiences that create a profound, undeniable biological connection."

He went on:

> "Pregnant women are not factories, and babies are not goods to be ordered. I believe this is crossing an ethical line, where human life is treated as a business transaction. The child must have the right to know where it has come from and how it came to be in this world. When a newborn is handed over not because of a tragedy, but because that separation was arranged before the child was even conceived, that child has become a commodity, which it should never be."

Mr Hinder told the House about Marie Anne, a British surrogate mother who described the moment after she gave birth: "They had the baby. They were happy. They didn't need me anymore so they told me to go home." She handed the baby over in a hospital car park and has since been diagnosed with complex post-traumatic stress disorder. Noting that France, Germany, Spain and Italy have banned surrogacy outright, he urged colleagues "to reject both the commodification of babies and mothers implied by the petition and the loosening of surrogacy laws in Britain".

## "Motherhood is certainly one of them"

[Steve Yemm](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-0AC09DA4-FA0A-4E4D-98F3-C557FB770E14), the Labour MP for Mansfield, said he recognised the "very understandable motivation" behind the petition but could not support it, and reframed the question before the House.

> "The question before us is not whether intended parents are real parents, nor whether they should ultimately receive legal recognition; the question is whether the woman who has carried and given birth to a child should lose her legal status as that child's mother from the moment of birth. I do not believe that she should. In our society, some things should never be reduced to questions of contract, individual choice or intention, and motherhood is certainly one of them. Pregnancy cannot simply be a service provided by one person for another, a woman's body cannot merely be the means by which someone else's parental intentions are fulfilled and the relationship created through nine months of pregnancy and childbirth cannot be written off because an agreement was reached beforehand."

On the six-week period before a parental order can be sought, he said: "Some describe that as outdated and difficult bureaucracy, but I describe it as a safeguard. Before conception, we cannot know with certainty how a woman will feel after pregnancy, childbirth and holding the baby whom she carried for the first time." He pointed out that in the first eleven months of 2025 there were 139 parental order applications for babies born through surrogacy in the UK against 357 for babies born abroad, and concluded:

> "The six-week period after birth, the mother's consent and independent judicial oversight are not antiquated obstacles to modern families, but protections for women and children—important safeguards that I believe are worth keeping."

## "Extreme caution"

[Rebecca Smith](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-17E905E2-9A04-4FF9-9187-8C9C19329C08), the Conservative MP for South West Devon, urged Members to approach the proposal "with extreme caution" and said the petition's framing of the parental order process as an obstacle to parental rights was "fundamentally misleading".

> "The legal process provides important protections for surrogate women and the children they carry. Currently, intended parents can apply for parental orders only after six weeks from the birth, and they must usually do so within six months. That cooling-off period provides a vital safeguard for the surrogate mother, and we should not dispense with it lightly."

She reminded the House that the UK is "an outlier in allowing any form of surrogacy at all", with the practice strictly limited or prohibited in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Sweden and Switzerland, and that the United Nations special rapporteur on violence against women and girls has recommended its global abolition. She warned that typical "expenses" paid to surrogates now reach £25,000: "if pregnancy becomes a source of substantial financial benefit, we need to ask whether we are still talking about altruism or whether we are creating a market in all but name."

> "It should concern us all that international surrogacy takes place in jurisdictions where the economic circumstances of surrogate mothers are very different from those in Britain. If wealthy countries normalise the commissioning of pregnancies, there will inevitably be markets that meet that demand. In most cases, the women with the least economic power become the people expected to take the greatest physical risks."

Ms Smith asked the Government to confirm that it remained committed to the parental order process: "Those safeguards are not outdated as the petition suggests; indeed, they are needed now more than ever."

## A Labour MP calls for a total ban

[Tracy Gilbert](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-4C2DC75F-A5FC-4485-B3D7-25853C645973), the Labour MP for Edinburgh North and Leith, said the debate was "long overdue" because "fundamental human rights" were at stake: the rights of women "not to be forced, coerced or trafficked to service the growing demand for surrogates", and the rights of children "to know where they came from, to have a nationality, and to be cared for by their parents".

She said the Law Commission's proposals would tip "the balance of power away from the rights of the birth mother", so that a woman who used her own egg, gave birth and changed her mind "would end up in a court battle in which the judge would decide who the parent or parents of the child are". Citing the international surrogacy industry's projected value of more than 200 billion dollars by 2032, she called on the Government to "fully reject the Law Commission's proposals" and went further than any other speaker:

> "Fifty per cent of responses to the Law Commission's consultation called for a total ban on surrogacy in the UK. I fully support such a ban, but it should be based on a wider debate that draws on evidence and focuses on reducing harm. In the interim, the Government must immediately recognise the harm and risk that is being inflicted on women and children through international surrogacy and take steps to cut off the UK's growing demand for it, as that is the only way to play our part in ending the coercion and trafficking of women and children. I understand people's desire to become parents, but that desire should not take priority over the rights of a child."

## "The child must come first"

[Shivani Raja](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-8E870579-0B4E-40BA-B4DD-0E98EBDEAA41), the Conservative MP for Leicester East, said she opposed the petition because automatic recognition "could remove important safeguards at precisely the moment when a child is most vulnerable".

> "We should begin with the most important person in this debate: the child. A child cannot consent to a surrogacy arrangement, and they cannot understand the promises adults have made. They cannot know what might happen if circumstances change—and circumstances can change."

She cited a 2023 Court of Appeal case in which a parental order was set aside and a surrogate mother who had used her own egg was awarded contact with the child four times a year, and a 2025 High Court case in which the intended parents "had never met the surrogate carrying the child, and did not even have information about her identity".

> "That six-week period is not a pointless delay; it is a breathing space and a safeguard. It recognises that giving birth to a child is not simply the completion of a contract, but a profound physical and emotional event, and that the law should allow time for circumstances and feelings to be properly considered."

"Compassion must never mean abandoning scrutiny," she concluded, "because when adults disagree, a child has to live with the consequences. That is why I cannot support automatic legal parenthood from birth. The child must come first."

## "Paying a woman for carrying a baby is quite another"

[Jim Shannon](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-6F03F3DB-B821-41D8-9155-D18C2CA8A647), the DUP MP for Strangford and his party's health spokesperson, said his concern was "urging caution to ensure that, while we need to legislate for the new scenarios that we face in modern life, protection is in place". He drew a sharp distinction between altruistic and commercial arrangements.

> "An important distinction must be made between the women who voluntarily agree to carry a child for someone they know, perhaps with reasonable expenses being covered, and the wholly exploitative system whereby a woman's womb becomes a service that can be bought. That concerns me greatly."

> "We must tread very carefully to not contribute to a market that preys on the financially vulnerable and pressures them to use their bodies for the benefit of others. Compensation for genuine expenses incurred in carrying a baby is one thing; paying a woman for carrying a baby is quite another."

Noting that commercial surrogacy is illegal in the UK but permitted abroad, "leading many UK couples to circumvent the law by using a surrogate abroad", he asked the Minister what was being done to close those loopholes. "Surrogacy should remain an incredible act of generosity," he said. "It cannot be anything else—not a commercial transaction that risks exploiting vulnerable women and commodifying their children. That can never happen."

[Phil Brickell](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-3A76514C-0334-482D-9E1C-30EA7955010A), the Labour MP for Bolton West, told the House that two of his constituents had travelled to Mexico for a surrogacy birth arranged by My Surrogacy Journey, a company listed on gov.uk, and had "repeated traumatic experiences" with it. He asked for the company to be removed from the Government's list pending a review by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority. The Minister later confirmed that the Department is "looking into the allegations" and will consider whether the company should remain listed.

## The Opposition front bench

Speaking for the Opposition, shadow minister [Dr Neil Shastri-Hurst](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/2026-09-07/debates/1DE05B16-B467-48D0-B6E7-6B7AD7F8B921/#contribution-5EEB8405-C487-4758-9EAE-AAF6BF258F2E), the Conservative MP for Solihull West and Shirley and a former member of the HFEA's appeals panel, said that any reform must protect the child, preserve "the free and continuing consent of the woman who gives birth" and address the safeguarding concerns raised by international commercial surrogacy.

> "Nobody doubts the love that such parents have for their children. Families formed through surrogacy deserve dignity, certainty and respect. However, our job in this House is to consider more than the wishes of the adults involved. We must speak for the child who cannot speak for themselves. We must also protect the woman who carries and gives birth to that child."

He rejected the argument that welfare checks are unnecessary because most applications succeed: "The fact that a system usually finds that everything is in order does not mean that the checks serve no purpose at all." And he warned what automatic parenthood would mean for a surrogate who changed her mind.

> "The woman who carried and delivered the child would instead have to take legal action if she wished to withdraw her consent or assert her own rights. That should concern us all, particularly where there is a financial or social imbalance between the surrogate mother and the intended parents. A system that depends on someone's having the knowledge, confidence and money to begin legal proceedings may offer very little protection in practice. Compassion for intended parents cannot require Parliament to treat the woman who gives birth as a temporary party to somebody else's story."

Dr Shastri-Hurst noted that parental order applications for children born overseas had risen from 215 in 2021 to 509 in 2025, against 150 for children born in the UK, and pressed the Government to update its 2022 overseas surrogacy guidance. "In surrogacy as in every other part of family law," he concluded, "the wishes of the adults are important but the welfare of children must always come first."

## The case for change

The petition's supporters were led by Dave Robertson, the Labour MP for Lichfield, who opened the debate for the Petitions Committee and introduced the petition's creator, Adam, whose daughter was born through surrogacy in the United States and who, six months on, is still not recognised as her legal parent in the UK. Josh Newbury (Labour, Cannock Chase), Rachel Taylor (Labour, North Warwickshire and Bedworth), Liberal Democrat spokesperson Jess Brown-Fuller (Chichester) and John Milne (Liberal Democrat, Horsham) argued that the Law Commission's proposed "new pathway", with screening before birth and a six-week window for the surrogate to object, would recognise intended parents from day one while keeping a safeguard in place. Ms Taylor said that "to suggest that the concept of consent can be questioned after consent has been given is dangerous".

Mr Robertson himself noted that the Law Commission's authors "never intended that part of their proposal to be carved out and delivered in isolation", that the new pathway would apply only to UK arrangements, and that the second most popular destination for UK surrogacy is Nigeria, "a country currently subject to special restrictions when it comes to adopting a child due to child welfare concerns".

## The Government's response

Dame Diana Johnson, the Minister for Public Health and Patient Safety, said the Government "recognise the arguments on both sides" and that the safeguarding assessments in the parental order process, "although [they] take time, are necessary to support the court's considerations". She told the House:

> "I hear loud and clear the calls for legislative change this afternoon but, given the limited parliamentary time available, the Government are not in a position to bring forward legislation on surrogacy reform immediately. We will, however, continue to consider options for future reform."

She added that "the welfare of children born through surrogacy must remain paramount" and that the Government would respond to the Law Commissions' 2023 report "in due course as time allows".
