### What are we asking for in the petition?

The Bundestag must reject any law, decree or Brussels-driven mandate that would force messaging services to scan, log or hand over the private communications of German citizens, because the promise of a free and unmonitored private life is not the state's to revoke by administrative shortcut. Members who sit on the Ausschuss für Recht und Verbraucherschutz owe the German people a clear, on-the-record refusal of chat control in every form it takes, whether client-side scanning, mandatory backdoors, or "voluntary" scanning schemes dressed up as child protection. Debate on this has already reached the floor and the committee; silence now would let a surveillance framework slide through unchallenged while everyone still believes it was stopped in Brussels.

### What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?

Every private conversation becomes a suspect's file: chat control does not target criminals, it scans the messages of every citizen, believer, parent and churchgoer before any wrongdoing is even alleged.

Faith and family conversations lose their privacy first: prayer chains, pastoral counsel, family disputes over a child's upbringing, all become data sitting in a state or corporate database, waiting to be misread or leaked.

A vague law becomes a tool against dissent: as Fabian Jacobi warned in the Bundestag, the underlying offence provisions are already "overstretched and vague," and expanding surveillance around them hands the state a blank cheque to decide whose speech is criminal.

Data meant to catch predators becomes a target for abuse: Helge Limburg himself admitted the real danger of prosecutorial data "landing systematically in the wrong hands," yet the answer on offer is to collect even more of it, not less.

Citizens are asked to trust a system with no accountability: once mass scanning is built into the infrastructure of everyday messaging, there is no meaningful oversight of who reads what, when, or why.

The precedent will not stay confined to Germany: a national or European chat control regime becomes the template every other government points to when it wants the same powers.

### What are some arguments supporting our point of view?

Free speech cannot survive constant surveillance: Andreas Audretsch was right that defending freedom of opinion is what democrats must do in this moment, and that defence collapses the instant every message is monitored before it is sent.

Mass scanning presumes guilt before any crime exists: chat control treats every citizen's private correspondence as evidence to be pre-emptively examined, inverting the basic principle that suspicion must come before surveillance, not after.

The state's own data record argues against expanding its reach: Helge Limburg's warning about prosecutorial data reaching "the wrong hands" is a case against collecting more sensitive personal data, not a case for it.

Vague legal definitions make abuse inevitable: Fabian Jacobi's description of the underlying incitement provisions as an "overstretched and vague rubber paragraph" shows how easily broad surveillance powers can be turned against legitimate speech, including religious and political conviction.

Free expression, not counter-propaganda or censorship, is the real defence against disinformation: as Ronald Gläser told the Bundestag, "the only weapon against disinformation is freedom of speech," and chat control substitutes surveillance for that freedom.

Families and churches rely on confidential communication to function: pastoral care, marriage counselling and family decision-making depend on conversations that are not logged, scanned or stored by a third party.

A regulatory framework already exists to police illegal content without mass scanning: platform accountability under existing rules can be enforced, as Carolin Wagner noted regarding the DSA, without turning every citizen's device into a monitoring point.

Protecting the space for debate protects everyone's freedom of speech, not just one side's: Catarina dos Santos-Wintz's point that the debate space must be protected "for our freedom of speech" cuts directly against any scheme that chills what citizens dare to say to one another privately.

Once built, the infrastructure of mass surveillance is never dismantled: technical scanning capability, once installed in every messaging app, remains available to any future government, regardless of the promises made when it was introduced.

### Why is it urgent that we take action now?

This proposal has already been referred (überwiesen) and is moving through the Ausschuss für Recht und Verbraucherschutz. There is no fixed deadline announced yet, but committee referral is precisely the stage at which quiet technical amendments get inserted with almost no public attention.

Once a chat control framework is adopted, whether at national or European level, it does not stay temporary. The scanning infrastructure becomes permanent plumbing inside every messaging service citizens use, and reversing it afterwards requires undoing embedded technical architecture, not simply repealing a clause.

This is the test of whether Germany's own MPs meant what they said on the Bundestag floor. Members from across the spectrum, from AfD to the Greens, have publicly warned against expanding state surveillance and defended freedom of opinion as a democratic duty. This is the moment that rhetoric is measured against a vote.

If citizens stay silent now, while the file sits in committee, lawmakers will read that silence as consent. Chat control has been defeated in public opinion before only because ordinary people spoke up loudly and early. Silence today hands the advantage entirely to those pushing mass surveillance through.

### Why would they listen to us?

Members across party lines, including AfD and Greens, have already gone on record in the Bundestag opposing expansions of state data collection and defending freedom of opinion. This is a live, contested vorgang still in committee, not a settled matter, which means MPs are actively weighing public reaction before deciding how the file moves forward. A clear, visible wave of constituent pressure now can tip members who have already voiced discomfort with surveillance overreach toward an open, on-the-record rejection of chat control.

### Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:

If the Bundestag allows chat control to advance unchallenged, every private message between spouses, parents, churchgoers and friends becomes a permanent record open to state access and vulnerable to leaks, exactly the kind of breach Helge Limburg himself warned could happen. Families lose the confidential space to discuss faith, conscience and conviction without wondering who is reading over their shoulder, and Germany becomes the precedent other governments cite to justify their own surveillance regimes.

### Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:

The Ausschuss für Recht und Verbraucherschutz and the full Bundestag reject chat control in every form, client-side scanning, mandatory backdoors and voluntary scanning schemes alike, and issue a clear statement that private communication between citizens will not be treated as evidence before any crime is alleged. Families, churches and ordinary citizens keep the confidential space they need to speak, pray and counsel freely, and Germany stands as the country that stopped mass surveillance before it became infrastructure.
