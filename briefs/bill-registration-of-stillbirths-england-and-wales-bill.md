**What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?**

Babies lost too early are legally invisible: current law recognises a stillbirth only when a baby is born after a set point in gestation, meaning countless babies lost earlier are given no formal registration, no certificate, no legal trace that they ever lived [VERIFY exact threshold]. Parents are denied the dignity of an official record: for many families, a birth and death certificate is the only lasting proof their child existed, and its absence deepens an already unbearable grief. The state's silence compounds private sorrow: a bureaucratic gap becomes a wound, telling parents in effect that their loss did not count in the eyes of the law. This Bill risks dying unheard at first reading: Private Members' Bills routinely stall without government support, and a compassionate, common-sense change to the law could simply be allowed to lapse. Every week of delay is another grieving family turned away: as debate continues elsewhere over where life begins and ends, families burying babies lost early in pregnancy are left waiting for basic recognition that should never have been in question. The wider culture of treating unborn life as disposable feeds this neglect: when the law hesitates to name a lost baby as a person who mattered, it reflects a broader failure to hold the unborn in proper regard.

## Prepare phase prompts

**What are some arguments supporting our point of view?**

Every life deserves to be named: recognising a stillbirth in law, whatever the stage of pregnancy, affirms that the child who died was a person, not a statistic to be filed away. Grief needs recognition, not paperwork gaps: bereaved parents have said for years that the absence of a certificate makes healing harder, turning private mourning into a fight for basic acknowledgement. This Bill is consistent, not radical: extending registration to babies currently excluded closes an anomaly in the law rather than creating a new right, and it should command support across the political spectrum. It reinforces the sanctity of life at every stage: a state that is willing to formally register a baby lost before the current threshold is a state taking a small but meaningful step toward consistency in valuing life from conception. It stands in stark contrast to moves to expand abortion up to birth: at a moment when some in Parliament seek to strip away protections for the unborn, this Bill offers a rare, humane acknowledgement that every baby's life and death matter. Other countries already do this: [VERIFY jurisdictions] have extended stillbirth registration further than England and Wales currently does, showing it is practical and workable. Compassion for the bereaved crosses political lines: this is not a partisan abortion fight, it is a chance for MPs of every party to show that grieving families are heard. Delay costs real families real pain: every month Parliament fails to move this Bill forward is another month of parents denied a certificate for the child they lost. A government that claims to care for families in crisis must prove it here: warm words about supporting bereaved parents mean little if a straightforward Bill to formalise their loss in law is left to wither.

**Why is it urgent that we take action now?**

This Bill is only at first reading in the Commons, with no date yet fixed for its next stage. That is precisely the danger: Private Members' Bills at this early point are fragile, and without visible public pressure and government backing they routinely disappear from the Order Paper altogether, never reaching a vote.

Once a Bill like this stalls, it does not simply pause, it dies. There is no guarantee of a future slot, no promise that another MP will pick up the cause, and grieving families will be left waiting indefinitely for a change in the law that should be straightforward and uncontroversial.

Successive governments have spoken of supporting bereaved parents and improving baby loss services, yet the law on registration has not kept pace. This Bill is a direct test of whether that concern is genuine or merely rhetorical.

If supporters stay silent now, at this earliest and most vulnerable stage, Parliament will read that silence as public indifference. We must act before the Bill quietly falls away, not after.

**Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:**

If the Government fails to back this Bill, it will fall away quietly after first reading, as so many Private Members' Bills do, and grieving parents across England and Wales will continue to be denied any legal record of the baby they lost. Their loss will remain, in the eyes of the state, as though it never happened, deepening the isolation of families already carrying unbearable grief.

**Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:**

If the Government gives this Bill the time and support it needs, Parliament will change the law so that many more babies lost in pregnancy are formally registered as stillborn, giving grieving parents the certificate and recognition they have long been denied, and affirming in law that every lost baby's life and death matter.
