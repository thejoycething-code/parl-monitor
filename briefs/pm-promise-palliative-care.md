## The three tests (the spine of the campaign)

The petition asks the Prime Minister to keep the promise he made on 29 July. It names three tests so that "fixing care" cannot be answered with a speech.

1. **Test 1, the Budget (28 October 2026).** Money for hospices that recurs, not another capital grant. Hospice UK's £112.5m a year is the starting measure because it is costed, public and already on the Health Secretary's desk (Wendy Morton's PQ 26030, answered 7 September). Alongside it, measures we can check on Budget day and in the weeks after, in rough order of how hard they bite:
   - **NHS contracts, not grants:** every ICB to move hospices onto multi-year contracts that pay the full cost of the clinical care commissioned (staff, medicines, estate, compliance), indexed to costs. The Framework can mandate this; the Budget can fund it.
   - **Pay parity pass-through:** each NHS pay award automatically funded for hospice clinical staff, so hospices stop losing nurses to the NHS over wages they cannot match.
   - **Children's Hospice Grant made permanent:** the £26m annual grant (2025/26) written into the baseline and uplifted, ring-fenced from local contracts, with the £34.3m 2026/27 shortfall closed.
   - **National Insurance:** compensate hospices for the April 2025 employer NICs rise as the NHS was compensated (Morton's PQ 26033 asked exactly this and got no figure).
   - **A statutory floor:** strengthen the palliative care duty on ICBs (Health and Care Act 2022, s.21) from "such services as the board considers appropriate" to services sufficient to meet assessed need, with a national accountability framework so provision stops depending on postcode.
   - **A right to be assessed:** a terminal diagnosis triggers a specialist palliative care assessment (Vikki Slade's PQ 27682, 11 September, asked for exactly this), so nobody dies unseen by a specialist because no one referred them.
   The petition names the first two and the children's grant; the rest go in the open letter and the report-back scorecard.
2. **Test 2, the Modern Service Framework (this autumn).** Publish it on time, with a funding line attached and a right to a specialist palliative care assessment for anyone with a terminal diagnosis, not a framework of aspirations.
3. **Test 3, the Casey report (summer 2027).** A funded plan for social care by the Prime Minister's own deadline, not another consultation.

Each test has a date, an owner and a public scorecard. That is what makes this a campaign and not a sentiment.

## Language guardrail (Christopher, 17 September)

Assisted suicide sits at the front of the copy because it is why our supporters care and why the promise exists. The copy must not, in any sentence, make good care a precondition for legalisation.

- Say: *Parliament has decided. The Prime Minister said care must come first. It is time to deliver it.*
- Say: *Good care is what a dying person is owed. Full stop.*
- Never say: "before assisted dying can be considered", "until care is fixed", "then we can have the debate", or anything that treats hospices as a waiting room for the next Bill.
- Never repeat the PM's line "I don't say that there shouldn't be a debate at some point" in our own voice; quote only the promise.
- Where the PM's own words imply a sequence, our framing is that the sequence has ended: care first was his test, and the Commons has removed the second step.
- Ministers who voted for the Bill are not "enemies of hospices" in our copy. Their votes are cited once, as fact, to explain why the promise needs watching.

We are asking the Prime Minister to keep the promise he made on 29 July 2026: to fix the funding of palliative and social care. Because the promise is his and the words are on the record. Because Parliament has just voted 286 to 270 in the direction he said the country needed to travel first, and a Prime Minister who abstained "so as not to unduly influence" MPs cannot now be seen to ignore what they decided. A new Prime Minister made fixing palliative and social care funding the condition for everything else on 29 July. Parliament rejected assisted suicide on 11 September. Nothing has yet been promised in money. If the promise is going to be kept, the next six weeks decide it.

**What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?**

A dying person in England has a one in four chance of not getting the specialist care they need, while the charities providing it close beds to balance the books. The state funds about forty pence in every pound of hospice care and leaves the rest to cake sales and legacies. The Prime Minister named this as the thing that had to be fixed first. If, having said that, the Government lets hospices go into the winter with no settled funding, the most vulnerable people in the country will have been used as an argument and then forgotten.

## Prepare phase prompts

**What are some arguments supporting our point of view?**

- The promise is specific and recent: "the fixing of the funding of palliative care and social care ... that's the position that I will stick to" (Prime Minister, 29 July 2026).
- The Prime Minister told Labour MPs on 28 August that his Government would implement the will of Parliament "whether that is to change the law or not". Parliament has spoken, 286 to 270.
- The numbers are the sector's, not ours: a £745m gap between what hospice care costs and what the state pays; nearly six in ten hospices cutting or considering cuts; 380 beds out of use; 150,000 fewer specialist home visits in a year.
- The ask is modest and costed: Hospice UK's £112.5m recurring first step is less than a tenth of one per cent of NHS England's budget, and the Government has already accepted the principle by promising a Modern Service Framework.
- This is where the country's compassion actually is: MPs on both sides of the 11 September vote, from Ashley Dalton to Liz Kendall, said in the same debate that palliative and social care must improve. There is no lobby against hospices, only a Treasury that has not yet been made to choose.
- The Government has done the capital and not the revenue: £125m for buildings and equipment since December 2024, nothing recurring for the nurses who staff them. Hospices cannot pay wages with a new roof.

**Why is it urgent that we take action now?**

The leader's speech is on 29 September and the Budget is on 28 October. Hospices set their 2027–28 staffing plans this autumn; Wendy Morton's written question of 1 September asked the Health Secretary precisely whether money would come "before hospices make staffing and service decisions for 2027–28". After the Budget, a promise not kept becomes a promise deferred to a Spending Review nobody has dated. The Modern Service Framework can be published with or without money; which version appears depends on the pressure applied in the next six weeks.

**Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:**

The conference speech praises carers and names no money. The Budget repeats the capital-only pattern or offers a one-off grant. The Modern Service Framework arrives as guidance with no funding line. Hospices cut further beds over the winter. The promise of 29 July quietly becomes a talking point about the Casey Commission in 2027, and the argument that "care must come first" is left lying on the ground for the next assisted suicide Bill's sponsors to pick up as proof that care will never be fixed.

**Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:**

The Prime Minister names hospices in Liverpool on 29 September and commits to funding them in the Budget. On 28 October the Chancellor announces recurring revenue funding for hospices and a move to NHS contracts that cover the cost of commissioned care. The Modern Service Framework is published this autumn with a right to a specialist assessment for everyone with a terminal diagnosis. CitizenGO supporters see the campaign they fought for turn into care for real people, and the record shows who kept their word.
