### What are we asking for in the petition?

The Bundestag must vote to repeal Germany's participation in the Digital Services Act and press the Federal Government to demand its dismantling at EU level, because no unelected commission in Brussels was ever owed the power to decide what a citizen may say online. Members who called themselves democrats in the same breath as they defended this law owe the country an honest reckoning: either they stand for free expression, as several of them have said aloud in the chamber, or they stand for a bureaucratic machine that fines platforms into silencing lawful speech before a judge ever sees it. The motion on the table gives them the chance to prove which one they meant.

### What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?

Brussels' silence machine punishes platforms, not proven crimes: the DSA lets regulators fine companies into removing content on suspicion alone, long before any court has ruled the speech unlawful, which means the safest business decision for a platform is always to delete first and ask questions never.

Vague categories invite political policing: terms like "disinformation" and "harmful content" have no fixed legal meaning, which hands enormous discretion to officials and NGOs who decide, case by case, whose views count as acceptable.

Ordinary citizens lose their voice with no real appeal: a post can vanish and an account can be throttled through an opaque algorithmic process, leaving the person who spoke with little practical recourse against a system built for scale, not for justice.

Enforcement power sits an ocean away from the voter: decisions that shape what a German citizen can say are made by a Commission in Brussels that no German voter elected and cannot remove, breaking the basic democratic promise that those who govern speech answer to those who are governed.

Even sympathetic voices admit the danger is real: one member warned openly in the chamber about prosecutorial data landing in the wrong hands, yet the answer offered was more regulation of the state's own surveillance, not less power for Brussels to police speech.

The law's defenders openly call for harsher enforcement: rather than narrowing the DSA's reach, some in government want "juicy fines" pursued further against major platforms, a direction that only deepens the chilling effect on what can safely be posted.

### What are some arguments supporting our point of view?

Free speech is the only real defence against propaganda: as one member put it plainly in debate, the answer to propaganda can never be counter-propaganda, because the single genuine weapon against disinformation is free expression itself, not a censorship regime.

The DSA outsources judgment to algorithms and pressure, not courts: content is removed or restricted at speed and at scale, a process that by its very design cannot weigh context, satire, dissent or minority opinion the way a courtroom can.

A "gute, wichtige Regelwerk" defended by fines against Meta and Google is still a law that reaches every citizen: supporters of the DSA point to penalties against big tech as proof of success, but every mechanism built to punish a platform's failure to censor eventually punishes the speech of the person who posted it.

Protecting the "Debattenraum" cannot mean shrinking it: several members insist they act "for" free expression rather than against it, yet a law that empowers takedown-on-suspicion narrows the space for debate precisely when it claims to protect it.

Sovereignty over speech belongs with elected, accountable lawmakers: Germany's own Bundestag, answerable to German voters, is better placed to judge the boundaries of lawful expression than a supranational body operating at arm's length from any ballot box.

Repeal does not mean lawlessness: existing German and European criminal law already covers genuine incitement and unlawful content, so scrapping the DSA removes a parallel, unaccountable enforcement layer without leaving citizens unprotected from actual crimes.

The chilling effect falls hardest on unpopular but lawful views: it is precisely dissenting, minority, faith-based and conservative voices, the ones least likely to have a legal team on retainer, who suffer first when platforms err on the side of deletion.

Data concerns compound the danger: warnings raised in the chamber about prosecutorial data reaching the wrong hands show that the infrastructure built to police speech carries risks well beyond censorship itself, risks the DSA does nothing to resolve.

### Why is it urgent that we take action now?

This motion is already before the Bundestag, with committee work under way and members on record staking out their positions. Every week the DSA remains in force, more takedown decisions are made under its rules, more precedent accumulates, and more of the digital public square is shaped by a framework that was never put to a direct democratic vote.

Once a regulatory regime like this becomes embedded, national bureaucracies and platforms structure their compliance systems around it, and the political cost of unwinding it only grows. Repeal becomes harder, not easier, with every passing month it is allowed to operate unchallenged.

Members across the chamber have already spoken about defending free expression "in this phase," acknowledging the stakes are immediate, not theoretical. That is the promise now being tested: will it be honoured with a vote, or will it remain a line used in debate while the law itself continues unchecked.

If citizens who value free speech stay silent now, while the motion is live and members are weighing their positions, that silence will be read in Berlin and Brussels alike as consent to a censorship framework that reaches into every household with an internet connection.

### Why would they listen to us?

This is a live motion with committee activity already logged and members from multiple parties on the record, which means the debate is genuinely open rather than settled. Voices across the political spectrum, including some who otherwise support tighter platform rules, have publicly invoked free expression as a democratic duty, showing there is real space to build a majority around repeal if enough constituents make their view known now, while positions are still being formed.

### Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:

If the Bundestag lets this motion fail or fade without a vote, Brussels keeps the power to force platforms into pre-emptive censorship of ordinary citizens, and every German who posts a view outside the approved consensus risks having it deleted, throttled or shadow-banned with no real appeal, while the officials responsible remain unaccountable to any voter.

### Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:

If the Bundestag votes to repeal Germany's participation in the Digital Services Act and pushes for its dismantling at EU level, ordinary citizens win back the basic right to speak, debate and dissent online without a distant bureaucracy deciding in advance what is safe to say, restoring free expression as a matter for courts and voters, not commissioners.

### Ideas for eventual Offline Actions

Organise a public letter-writing evening where supporters draft personal messages to their Bundestag representatives, timed to land while the motion is active in committee. Hold a short doorstep or town-square information stall with printed copies of the key quotes from the debate, letting passers-by see in members' own words what is being defended and what is at risk. Coordinate with sympathetic local faith and family groups to raise the issue at existing meetings, turning attendees into constituents who write, call or visit their MP's office in person.
