**What are we asking for in the petition?**

We call on the House of Lords, in this short debate, to declare plainly that no age of childhood can make a child's agreement to puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones genuine, informed consent, and to press the Government to close every door that still allows this to happen to minors in the United Kingdom. Peers owe this to the children already harmed and to the families who trusted that medicine would do no harm. A child cannot weigh the loss of fertility, the risk to bone density, the unknown long-term effects on a developing brain, against the promise of relief from distress; no amount of counselling or paperwork changes that basic truth. The Lords must say so without hedging, and must ask ministers directly what further safeguards, bans and reviews will follow from this admission.

**Why would they listen to us?**

Peers in the House of Lords have shown they will listen when the evidence is put before them plainly, as demonstrated by the move towards restricting puberty blockers following the Cass Review's findings. A clear, well-evidenced public voice reinforces the case that caution, not haste, is what the public and the medical evidence both demand, and reminds wavering peers that silence on this question will not go unnoticed by the families and children affected.

**What is happening that we are responding to?**

Lords debate on consent age for puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones goes to the heart of paediatric transition safeguards.

**What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?**

Children are being asked to consent to what adults cannot fully explain: Puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones carry effects on fertility, bone density and brain development that remain genuinely uncertain, yet children are presented with paperwork as though they were choosing a hairstyle, not a medical pathway with lifelong consequences.

The law has already moved, but the principle has not been settled: The puberty blocker ban was a step forward, yet without a clear parliamentary statement on the very idea of a "consenting age" for these interventions, clinics and pressure groups retain room to argue that some children, somewhere, are old enough.

A debate without teeth risks becoming a box ticked: A short Lords debate can pass without a single safeguard tightened, while campaigners point to it afterwards as proof that Parliament has "considered the matter" and move on.

Families are left to fight alone: Parents who raise concerns about affirmation-only approaches in schools and clinics are too often treated as the problem, not the children who deserve protection from irreversible medical pathways pushed before they can possibly understand them.

The vulnerable are targeted hardest: Children struggling with distress, autism, trauma or simply the ordinary turbulence of adolescence are being fast-tracked towards hormonal intervention rather than given the patient, non-ideological care they are owed.

## Prepare phase prompts

**What are some arguments supporting our point of view?**

No child can consent to an unknown future: Medicine itself does not know the full long-term effects of puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones on children's bodies and minds, so no child, however articulate, can give consent that is genuinely informed.

The Cass Review exposed the evidence gap: Clinical reviews of gender medicine for children found a worryingly thin evidence base behind treatments that were being offered as settled and safe, a gap that demands caution, not expansion.

Consent age for life-altering hormones should never be negotiable: Where Parliament allows any debate about a "consenting age" for irreversible medical intervention, it implicitly accepts that some number of years makes the irreversible acceptable, and that premise must be rejected outright.

Safeguarding law already treats children as unable to consent to serious harm: The law protects children from tattoos, from marriage, from countless decisions far less consequential than altering their hormonal development, and gender medicine deserves no exception.

Detransitioners' testimony cannot be ignored: Growing numbers of young people who once identified as trans and later regretted irreversible treatment describe being rushed through a process that should have slowed down, not sped up, at the first sign of distress.

Other countries are retreating from this approach: Jurisdictions that once led on paediatric gender medicine are now restricting or reviewing these treatments precisely because the evidence did not support rapid medicalisation of children.

Schools and clinics should not be battlegrounds for ideology: Children are entitled to care rooted in caution and established medical evidence, not in a contested ideological framework that treats scepticism as bigotry.

**Why is it urgent that we take action now?**

This short debate in the House of Lords is the moment for peers to go on record, and once a debate like this closes without a firm conclusion, the window to shape the official position narrows sharply until the next opportunity arises, which may be months away.

If Parliament allows the question of a "consenting age" to remain open or vague, that vagueness becomes the working assumption inside clinics and gender services, where ambiguity has repeatedly been read as permission to proceed.

This debate tests whether the puberty blocker ban was a genuine commitment to protecting children or a partial measure that leaves the door ajar. Ministers and peers who supported restricting these treatments for children owe it to the public to confirm, unambiguously, that no age makes consent to irreversible hormonal intervention valid for a minor.

Silence from concerned voices during this debate will be read by campaigners on the other side as acceptance that the matter is settled in their favour. Supporters who care about this must make their voices heard now, while peers are actively considering the question, not after the debate has closed and the moment has passed.

**Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:**

If the House of Lords allows this debate to close without a clear statement that no child can meaningfully consent to puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones, the door stays open for clinics, campaigners and future ministers to argue that some children are old enough, and more children will be put on an irreversible medical pathway before they can possibly understand what it will cost them.

**Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:**

The House of Lords states clearly that no age of childhood can make consent to puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones genuine, and ministers are pressed into confirming firm, lasting safeguards that keep children away from irreversible medical intervention until they are adults capable of deciding for themselves.
