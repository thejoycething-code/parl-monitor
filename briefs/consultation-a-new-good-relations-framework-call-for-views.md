**What are we asking for in the petition?**

The Executive Office of Northern Ireland must guarantee, in black and white, that the new Good Relations Framework cannot be used to police what people believe or say about marriage, family, faith and conscience. Ministers have invited views precisely because this framework will shape how "good relations" is defined and enforced for years to come, and that promise of genuine public consultation is only honoured if dissenting Christian, family-focused and traditional voices are protected as fully as any other. The Executive Office owes the people of Northern Ireland a framework that fosters real harmony between communities, not one that quietly brands orthodox belief a threat to be managed and marginalised.

**Why would they listen to us?**

This is formally a call for views, meaning the Executive Office has explicitly invited public submissions and cannot claim it did not know what ordinary people think. A strong, clear volume of responses defending freedom of conscience and free speech is difficult for officials to ignore without appearing to have consulted in bad faith. Past campaigns on related issues, from protecting Christian teaching in schools to resisting ideological conformity in public training, have shown that concerted public pressure in Northern Ireland moves decision-makers to reconsider vague or one-sided drafting.

**What is happening that we are responding to?**

A New Good Relations Framework - Call For Views. Deadline: 2026-09-21.

**What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?**

"Good relations" could become a licence to silence dissent: A framework built to ease community tensions risks being turned into a tool that treats traditional views on marriage and family as the problem to be solved, rather than a legitimate perspective to be respected.

Christian conviction could be recast as a public order issue: Once "good relations" duties are written into policy and practice, teachers, employers and public bodies may feel pressured to treat orthodox religious belief as something requiring correction rather than accommodation.

Consultation without real listening breeds resentment, not harmony: If this call for views becomes a box-ticking exercise, communities who feel unheard will trust the process less, not more, undermining the very reconciliation the framework claims to seek.

Free speech could be sacrificed for the appearance of peace: Genuine good relations require the freedom to disagree well. A framework that quietly narrows what can be said in the name of avoiding offence achieves conformity, not community.

Families and churches could be left without a voice in shaping the rules that govern them: Those most affected by any new "good relations" duties, parents, pastors and everyday believers, risk being sidelined in favour of institutional and activist voices with louder megaphones.

Northern Ireland's hard-won peace could be misused to justify ideological conformity: The language of reconciliation, built for a very different purpose, must not be repurposed to demand agreement on contested moral and social questions.

## Prepare phase prompts

**What are some arguments supporting our point of view?**

Good relations must mean mutual respect, not enforced agreement: True reconciliation allows people to hold different beliefs about marriage, family and faith while still living peaceably together. A framework that demands uniformity of opinion is not building good relations, it is building a single approved opinion.

Freedom of conscience is foundational, not optional: Any framework governing how communities relate to one another must start from the premise that people are free to believe, teach and live according to their conscience without being treated as a source of "bad relations".

Vague definitions invite selective enforcement: Without clear, written protections for religious and moral conviction, terms like "good relations" can be stretched to justify silencing whoever is currently unpopular, and history shows that is rarely a fair or consistent standard.

Public consultations only work if all voices are genuinely weighed: A call for views is a democratic promise. If traditional and faith-based responses are treated as noise to be managed rather than legitimate input, the process becomes a formality rather than a fair hearing.

Northern Ireland's peace process was about accommodation, not erasure: The Good Friday framework succeeded because it made room for different identities and convictions to coexist. A new good relations policy must honour that spirit rather than quietly narrowing what beliefs are acceptable to hold in public life.

Churches, schools and families need certainty, not creeping restriction: Institutions that shape the next generation deserve clear assurance that teaching biblical views on life, marriage and family will not be treated as a breach of "good relations" duties.

Free speech and good relations are not opposites: Some of the healthiest societies are those where robust, respectful disagreement is protected, not suppressed. A framework that conflates disagreement with division gets the diagnosis wrong.

What is written into public policy now shapes enforcement for years: Once adopted, a Good Relations Framework becomes the reference point for guidance, training and complaints procedures across public bodies, so vague or one-sided drafting now becomes lived restriction later.

**Why is it urgent that we take action now?**

The call for views closes on 21 September 2026. After that date, the window for the public, including people of faith and traditional conviction, to shape this framework directly will close, and officials will move to drafting a settled policy.

Once adopted, a Good Relations Framework does not stay a discussion document. It becomes the reference point that public bodies, schools and employers turn to when writing guidance and handling complaints, which means whatever gets locked in now will govern how "good relations" is interpreted and enforced for years to come.

This consultation is, in effect, a test of whether the Executive Office's promise of genuine public engagement means anything. If it produces a framework that reflects only one set of voices, that promise will have been broken in practice even while being kept in name.

Silence now would be read as consent to whatever definition of "good relations" officials choose to write. Every response that goes unsent is a response the drafters never have to answer.

**Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:**

The Executive Office of Northern Ireland adopts a Good Relations Framework that treats traditional Christian belief about marriage, family and life as a threat to be managed rather than a conviction to be respected. Teachers self-censor for fear of breaching vague "good relations" duties, churches face pressure to soften their teaching in public settings, and families who hold orthodox views find themselves quietly pushed to the margins of public life in the very province built on the promise of accommodation.

**Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:**

The Executive Office of Northern Ireland publishes a Good Relations Framework that explicitly protects freedom of conscience, religious belief and free speech, stating plainly that disagreement over marriage, family and moral questions is not a breach of good relations but a normal feature of a free society. Christian parents, teachers and churches can speak, teach and live according to their convictions without fear of being labelled a threat to community harmony.
