### What are we asking for in the petition?

The European Commission must reject every path toward EU-wide funding, promotion or "harmonisation" of abortion access, and must state plainly that abortion policy remains the sole competence of member states under the Treaties. This is not a technicality. The Commission holds a duty, promised in the founding architecture of the Union itself, to respect that health and moral questions of this magnitude belong to national parliaments and to the people who elect them, not to unelected officials in Brussels responding to a citizens' initiative with an agenda. Every member state, including the UK's neighbours who still hold the line for the unborn, is owed that respect. The Commission must not let "My Voice, My Choice" become Brussels' voice, Brussels' choice, imposed on nations that have not consented.

### What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?

Democratic consent is being bypassed: A citizens' initiative gathered signatures under a slogan of choice, yet the outcome sought is the opposite of choice for the nations being pressured, since it would strip elected national parliaments of the power to set their own abortion laws.

National sovereignty on life issues is under direct threat: Abortion law has always sat with member states precisely because it touches conscience, culture and faith in ways no single European framework can honestly represent.

The unborn have no voice in this "voice" campaign: A petition named for giving women a voice offers no voice at all to the child whose life is ended, and an EU response that treats this silence as unimportant compounds the injustice.

Precedent, once set, cannot be undone: If the Commission signals that EU-level competence can be stretched to cover abortion, that door does not close again, and it will be used to erode protections in every member state that still defends life.

Faith communities are being sidelined: Millions of European citizens hold pro-life convictions rooted in their faith, yet this initiative and the Commission's handling of it treat those convictions as obstacles to be managed rather than views to be respected.

Smaller and more conservative states are being pressured into conformity: Nations with strong protections for the unborn face reputational and political pressure to fall in line with a Brussels consensus they never voted for.

### What are some arguments supporting our point of view?

The Treaties are clear: Health policy, and specifically matters of criminal and moral law such as abortion, remain explicitly reserved to member states, and the Commission has no mandate to legislate around that reservation through the back door of a citizens' initiative response.

Citizens' initiatives are not laws: The European Citizens' Initiative mechanism exists to prompt debate, not to dictate outcomes, and the Commission owes it to every member state to treat "My Voice, My Choice" as one voice among many, not a mandate.

Subsidiarity exists for exactly this reason: The principle that decisions should be taken as close to citizens as possible was built to protect precisely these deeply held, culturally rooted questions from top-down imposition.

Abortion is not a settled European value: Member states hold sharply different laws and convictions on abortion, from near-total restriction to liberal access, and no single EU position can honestly claim to represent that diversity.

The child's right to life is a right too: Any EU document responding to this initiative must reckon with the fact that abortion ends a human life, a reality that "access" language works hard to obscure.

Funding pressure is coercion by another name: If the Commission moves toward EU funding mechanisms tied to abortion access, states that restrict abortion could face financial or political pressure to change their laws, which is coercion dressed as harmonisation.

Faith and conscience protections must not be an afterthought: Millions of doctors, nurses and citizens across Europe hold conscientious objections to involvement in abortion, and any EU-level push on this issue risks treating their convictions as a problem to be solved.

The Commission answers to the member states, not the other way round: Ultimate authority on issues this fundamental rests with national governments and parliaments, and the Commission must not use this initiative to reverse that order.

This is a test of whether Brussels respects difference: A Union that claims to celebrate diversity among its members cannot simultaneously push a single abortion policy onto all of them.

### Why is it urgent that we take action now?

The Commission's formal communication responding to this citizens' initiative is now in motion, and once a Commission position is published it becomes the reference point for every future push toward EU-level abortion policy, however cautiously it is worded today.

Frameworks like this do not stay modest. A communication that leaves the door even slightly open to EU competence over abortion access will be cited, expanded and built upon in future proposals, funding rounds and court cases, long after this specific initiative is forgotten.

This is a direct test of the EU's own founding promise that health and moral policy of this kind belongs to member states. If the Commission treats that promise as negotiable now, in response to pressure from a single campaign, it signals that the promise was never really binding.

Silence at this stage will be read as consent. If pro-life citizens and organisations do not make their voice heard in this consultation period, the Commission and the initiative's organisers will present that silence as proof that only one side of Europe was paying attention.

### Why would they listen to us?

The Commission is legally bound by the principle of subsidiarity and cannot simply ignore member states' sovereignty on abortion law without inviting direct legal and political challenge from national governments. A visible, well-documented wave of public objection from across Europe, including the UK's own pro-life community, adds real political cost to any move toward overreach, and Commission officials do track and cite the volume and geographic spread of responses when framing their formal communication.

### Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:

The European Commission publishes a communication that edges toward treating abortion access as an EU-level concern, giving future proposals, funding streams and legal challenges a foothold they did not have before. Member states that protect the unborn find themselves under mounting pressure to conform, and the voice of millions of faith-driven citizens who signed no such initiative is drowned out by a slogan that never spoke for them.

### Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:

The European Commission responds to "My Voice, My Choice" by firmly restating that abortion law is, and remains, the sole competence of member states, closing the door on any EU-level funding or harmonisation push. National parliaments retain the power to protect the unborn according to their own laws and convictions, and Brussels is put on notice that it does not get the final say on the most fundamental question of all: who counts as worthy of protection.

### Ideas for eventual Offline Actions

Organise a letter-writing drive to MEPs and to the relevant Commissioner asking them to publicly affirm member state competence on abortion. Coordinate with sister pro-life organisations across Europe to submit a joint statement during the Commission's consultation period. Hold a small prayer vigil or witness outside the European Commission's UK representation office. Distribute simple explainer cards at churches outlining what the ECI is and why "My Voice, My Choice" does not speak for everyone. Encourage supporters to write to their own MEPs asking them to raise the subsidiarity concern directly in Parliament.
