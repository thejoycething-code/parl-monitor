### What are we asking for in the petition?

The Bundesregierung must reject outright any recommendation from the Commission on Reproductive Self-Determination that would open the door to surrogacy, whether commercial or so-called "altruistic", and must hold the line on Germany's existing protections for children and mothers rather than let a summary report become the quiet first step towards rewriting them. Germany has long recognised that a child is not a product to be commissioned and a mother's body is not a service to be purchased or bartered, and this duty of protection, already written into German law and defended in the courts, is owed to every child born and every woman who might otherwise be pressured into carrying a child for others. The Bundesregierung must say clearly, now, before any legislation is drafted from this report, that this line will not move.

### What is the key point of injustice that is at stake here?

Children risk becoming contractual outcomes: A report built to "modernise" reproductive law treats the creation of a child as a transaction to be smoothed and regulated, not a life to be protected from the moment of conception.

Surrogate mothers face renewed pressure to be used as vessels: Even where surrogacy is dressed up as "altruistic", the woman carrying the child can be placed under financial, emotional or family pressure that no paperwork can undo, and Susanne Hierl's own remarks in the Bundestag debate confirm that abusive surrogacy already troubles lawmakers across parties.

Legal parentage is being rewritten to erase the reality of a child's origins: Calls to overhaul Abstammungsrecht (descent law), voiced openly by SPD and Green speakers in the same debate, would allow legal fictions to replace the biological truth of who a child's parents are, unsettling the very foundation the law exists to record.

The courts are already straining under the weight of unresolved cases: The Constitutional Court's pending Vorlagen and Verfassungsbeschwerde on so-called "co-motherhood" (case 1 BvL 1/21) show that Germany's family law is already being pulled in directions parliament has not properly debated or decided.

A government-commissioned report can quietly become government policy: Because the Bundesregierung itself brought this commission into being, its recommendations carry a weight that can bypass full public scrutiny and slide straight into future legislation unless supporters speak now.

Debate is already split along lines that favour drift, not protection: With Green and SPD voices pushing hard for change and no clear date yet fixed for full parliamentary consideration, silence from families and faith communities leaves the field open to those pressing for looser rules.

### What are some arguments supporting our point of view?

Surrogacy commodifies both mother and child: Whatever language is used to soften it, paying or pressuring a woman to carry and surrender a child treats her body and the child's life as goods to be exchanged, a moral line Germany has rightly refused to cross.

Germany's existing ban reflects hard-won moral clarity: The prohibition on surrogacy did not appear by accident; it followed serious reflection on the dignity of women and children, and a summary report should not be allowed to unpick that reflection without equally serious public debate.

The Commission's framing already tilts towards "reproductive self-determination" over child protection: A commission named for self-determination is structurally more likely to recommend expanding access to reproductive technologies than to defend the limits that protect the vulnerable, and its "likely" direction, as flagged in official monitoring, should worry anyone who values those limits.

Rewriting descent law would sever the link between biology and legal parenthood: Susanne Hierl's warning against loosening rules, set against Helge Lindh's call to "get to grips with Abstammungsrecht", shows parliament is already divided on whether a child's legal parents must bear any relation to their biological origin.

Exploitation is not hypothetical: it is already being legislated against elsewhere: Hierl noted that the government is tightening human trafficking law specifically to capture forced marriage, illegal adoption and abusive surrogacy, proof that lawmakers themselves recognise the exploitation risk surrogacy carries.

Constitutional uncertainty is already destabilising family law: With the Constitutional Court weighing multiple referrals on co-motherhood, adding a fresh push to loosen surrogacy and reproductive medicine rules risks compounding legal confusion rather than resolving it responsibly.

A report is not a mandate: Because no vote, reading or formal decision has yet taken place on this report's recommendations, there remains a real window for the Bundesregierung to set a clear, protective course before any bill is drafted.

Cross-party voices already share this concern: CDU/CSU's own contribution to the debate shows the ban on surrogacy and the case for closing exploitation loopholes is not a fringe position but one with real parliamentary support to build on.

### Why is it urgent that we take action now?

No formal date has yet been set for the Bundestag to take up this report's recommendations in full, but summary reports commissioned directly by the Bundesregierung are precisely the documents that quietly become the first draft of future legislation. The longer this report sits unopposed, the more its assumptions, that reproductive self-determination should trump existing protections, harden into the accepted starting point for any bill that follows.

Once a framework like this is adopted into legislative drafting, reversing it becomes far harder than preventing it. Germany's surrogacy ban and its child protection standards were built over years of careful debate; a report can undo the public argument for them in a single summary, long before any vote is taken.

This is a direct test of Germany's stated commitment to protecting children and preventing exploitation, a commitment CDU/CSU restated on the record in June 2026 when defending tighter trafficking law against abusive surrogacy. If the Bundesregierung now allows this Commission's recommendations to proceed unchallenged, that commitment will have been hollow.

Silence now will be read as consent. With Green and SPD members already pushing hard in debate for looser rules on descent law and reproductive medicine, and no organised opposition yet voiced from families and faith communities, every day without pushback strengthens the hand of those steering this report towards legislation.

### Why would they listen to us?

The Bundestag debate itself shows this is not settled ground: CDU/CSU has already spoken up for the surrogacy ban and against exploitation, giving supporters a sympathetic bloc to reinforce rather than persuade from scratch. Because no vote or formal decision has yet been taken on the Commission's recommendations, MPs and ministers still have room to shape the outcome, and public pressure now, before a bill is drafted, carries far more weight than pressure applied after the fact.

### Describe a bad outcome if we do not win this campaign:

The Bundesregierung quietly folds the Commission's recommendations into future legislation, surrogacy gains a legal foothold in Germany in some form, and the country's descent law is rewritten to erase the biological link between a child and their parents. Vulnerable women face renewed pressure to carry children for others, and children born of these arrangements grow up with their identity and origins treated as negotiable.

### Describe a good outcome if we do win this campaign:

The Bundesregierung publicly rejects the Commission's push to loosen surrogacy and reproductive medicine rules, reaffirms Germany's ban on commercial and altruistic surrogacy, and commits to strengthening rather than weakening the protections around a child's legal parentage. Families across Germany see their government choose the dignity of mothers and children over the demands of a report built to normalise commodifying them.

Encourage local pastors and community leaders to raise the issue in sermons or newsletters around key dates when the report is discussed in parliament. Arrange a small delegation to hand in a printed copy of the petition signatures at the Bundestag or a relevant ministry office once numbers are strong.
